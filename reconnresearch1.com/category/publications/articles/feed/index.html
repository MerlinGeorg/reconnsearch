Despite great resistance from external powers, Tehran succeeded in spreading its influence on Iraq, Syria, Lebanon, Yemen, and elsewhere in the region. The regime’s de facto control of much of the Middle East is nothing short of remarkable.

 

Despite crippling sanctions, the country is arguably stronger than at the time of their introduction. Iran has not only grown its political influence in neighboring states but has also enhanced its military capabilities in a direct challenge to adversaries like Israel, Saudi Arabia, and the United States. International pressure, which culminated in the 2015 Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action, sought to curb Iran’s nuclear ambitions in exchange for sanctions relief. The consensus holds that Iranian clout is an immovable fixture of Middle Eastern geopolitics.

 

Yet recent developments should make us reconsider Iran’s supposed strength. The killing of Quds Force Commander General Qasem Suleimani, along with Kataib Hezbollah Commander Abu Mahdi al-Muhandis, is a case in point.

 

The question here is: How did the United States infiltrate the Iranian front so easily and eliminate two of its most prominent leaders? For the regime in Tehran, this was an embarrassing failure.

 

Iranian futility did not stop at the targeted killings. The response was more symbolic than effectual, as Iran carried out a limited strike on the Ain Al-Assad base in Iraq that injured no Americans. Iraqi diplomatic sources told The Independent that Iran had coordinated with the United States through Gulf intermediaries and that both sides had acted to prevent casualties.

 

In reality, Tehran appears more interested in spreading propaganda than taking revenge on the United States. For instance, Iranian Foreign Minister Javad Zarif said following the strike that “Iran will suffice by this reply.” What an odd statement from a regime that has liberally killed American soldiers in the past. This and the leaks to The Independent indicate that Iran has no intent to escalate its confrontation with the United States.

 

Suleimani’s funeral served as another example of Iranian mismanagement and disorganization. The funeral procession in southeast Tehran caused a stampede that killed dozens and injured many more. This sorry spectacle stands at odds with the image of power, organization, and efficiency that Iran projects internationally. The regime’s true nature is one of weakness, not strength.

 

Things did not stop there. The day after the funeral, Tehran mistakenly shot down a Ukrainian plane, killing all 176 on board. The Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps officials in charge had assumed it was a hostile aircraft and initially denied having anything to do with the accident. Only later did President Hassan Rouhani admit that the downing of the airliner was an “unforgivable mistake” and announce imminent investigations.

 

Apart from the floundering of Iranian media that reported on contradictory statements from Tehran, the accident revealed shortcomings in the country’s missile defense. Operational since last August, the Bavar-373 missile system has been touted by the regime as on par with the Russian S-400 and the American MIM-104 Patriot systems. This came just two months after the unveiling of the Khordad 15 system, which Tehran boasts is similarly advanced.

 

A telling contradiction emerges. How can a country that, according to its own claims, owns such a sophisticated missile defense apparatus fail to distinguish between a civilian airliner and a hostile plane? Another sad irony is that Iran must now pay hundreds of millions in compensation to the victims’ families at a time when sanctions have squeezed its economy to a breaking point.

 

We in Kuwait should monitor this situation closely. Although Tehran is not nearly as strong as it would like the world to think, tensions have the potential to worsen. As a small and peaceful country, Kuwait has a vital interest in securing a stable Gulf and broader Middle East. The alternative is far from desirable.
